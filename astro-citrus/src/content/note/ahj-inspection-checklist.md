*Local rules win. If your AHJ tells you something that conflicts with this checklist, their requirement is the one that matters.*

## What "AHJ" Actually Means

AHJ stands for **Authority Having Jurisdiction** — the organization, office, or individual with the legal power to say "yes, you can build this" (permit approval) and "yes, this is installed correctly" (inspection sign-off). For solar and battery systems, your AHJ is usually one or more of:

  * **Building department** — structure, roof loading, general building code
  * **Electrical inspector** — wiring, overcurrent protection, labeling, NEC compliance
  * **Fire department / fire marshal** — access, clearances, battery fire behavior, emergency response

## Who Your AHJ Is

You don't pick your AHJ — it's defined by where the project is located. For most home or small commercial solar + battery projects, expect a city or county building department as the primary AHJ, with the local fire authority often reviewing battery locations separately.

**Quick ways to identify your AHJ:**

  * Check existing permit documents on the property — the header usually names the department (e.g., "City of Springfield Building Department")
  * Search "[your city] building permits" or "[your county] electrical inspector"
  * Ask your installer — any legitimate installer in your area knows which AHJ(s) they're dealing with
  * In rural or unincorporated areas, the AHJ is often the county rather than a city

## How to Contact Your AHJ

You don't need code section numbers — just clear, specific questions.

**Step 1 — find the right department.** Look for "Building & Safety," "Community Development," "Planning & Development Services," or "Fire Prevention" on your city/county website, then find the phone number or email for "inspections" or "plan review."

**Step 2 — ask targeted questions.** For a battery project:

  * Do you allow homeowner-installed (DIY) systems if a licensed electrician signs off on the final connections?
  * Do you have specific rules on where batteries can be installed (garage vs. exterior wall, minimum clearances)?
  * Do you require separate fire department review for batteries?

Write down the answers or save the email — if an inspector on site says something different later, you can refer back to what you were told.

## How to Use This Checklist

  * As a pre-inspection tool before your AHJ visit
  * As a one-pager to hand an installer or electrician: "please install it so this whole list is a 'yes' for our AHJ"
  * As a reference to link in project docs, proposals, and design notes

## The Checklist

### Rapid-Shutdown Compliance (NEC Article 706)

  * ☐ Rapid-shutdown label visible and legible (red label stating "RAPID SHUTDOWN SWITCH")
  * ☐ Main disconnect switch accessible within 10 feet, clearly labeled
  * ☐ Disconnect de-energizes the entire battery system when triggered
  * ☐ Electrician confirms switch operation

### Electrical Safety (NEC Articles 690, 750)

  * ☐ Arc-fault protection installed on all DC circuits (AFCI breakers or module-level AFCI)
  * ☐ Ground-fault protection on all DC circuits (GFDI or GFDI-protected combiner)
  * ☐ Battery disconnects are rated for DC current (AC-rated disconnects fail in DC)
  * ☐ All wire gauge is correct per system voltage and continuous current rating
  * ☐ Grounding is continuous and tested (resistance < 25 ohms)

### Fire & Life Safety

  * ☐ Battery enclosure has 36-inch clearance on all sides (ventilation, inspection, fire access)
  * ☐ No flammable materials within 6 feet
  * ☐ Ventilation holes aren't blocked
  * ☐ Temperature sensor installed and operational (if battery is indoors)

### Permits & Documentation

  * ☐ Permit number posted on electrical panel
  * ☐ One-line diagram matches actual installation
  * ☐ Equipment cut-sheets filed with permit
  * ☐ Installer certification on file

## Common Reasons AHJ Inspections Fail

  * **Plans don't match reality.** The inspector compares the plan to the house and finds the battery or disconnects in different locations. *Fix: update the plans before inspection, or install exactly where you said you would.*
  * **Missing or wrong labels.** Labeling is boring but easy to fail. *Fix: use labels matching your AHJ's expectations — many inverter/battery manufacturers provide label kits.*
  * **Battery installed where the AHJ doesn't allow it.** A classic one: battery in an attached garage where the AHJ wants it outside or behind a rated wall. *Fix: ask about acceptable locations before you drill holes or mount hardware.*
  * **DIY work without required licensed sign-off.** Some AHJs allow owner-pulled permits; others require a licensed electrician on the permit. *Fix: ask explicitly whether DIY is allowed and under what conditions.*

## Notes for Installers and Designers

Attach this checklist as an appendix to your design packet, and highlight any items specific to your local AHJ (e.g., "our city requires X clearance in garages"). Consider adding a client sign-off page where the homeowner initials that they understand the shutdown procedure and basic maintenance.
