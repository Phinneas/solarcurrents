# California breaks down barriers: Legislature sends balcony solar bill to governor

**The Bottom Line:** California's legislature passed [SB 868](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billNavClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260SB868), eliminating utility interconnection agreements for plug-and-play solar systems up to 1,200W. Renters and apartment dwellers can now buy panels at retailers like IKEA, plug them into standard outlets, and start cutting electricity costs without utility permission. The bill awaits Governor Newsom's signature after passing the Senate 35-1.

**The Story:** For families trapped in California's affordability crisis (where electricity rates doubled over the past decade), balcony solar represents direct action. A $500 system covering 14% of an apartment's electricity saves roughly $250 annually. That money matters when [extreme heat is up 55% statewide](https://www.ewg.org/news-insights/news-release/2026/08/ewg-californias-extreme-heat-soaring-utility-bills-make-cooling) and running AC means choosing between cooling and eating. 

Electrical workers unions broke ranks with utilities on this fight, going neutral after initial opposition. "We now have a set minimum standard for the safe installation of these things that won't put people at risk," said John Doherty of the California State Association of Electrical Workers. His union dropped opposition to a technology that puts clean energy directly in tenants' hands.

**By the numbers:**

- Over 4 million balcony solar systems installed in Germany, proving the model works at scale ([EWG](https://www.ewg.org/news-insights/news-release/2026/08/california-legislature-approves-bill-easing-access-clean))
- Systems cost around **$500** today, with prices expected to fall as California's market opens ([EWG](https://www.ewg.org/news-insights/news-release/2026/08/california-legislature-approves-bill-easing-access-clean))
- A 400W system saves approximately **$250 per year** for average apartment electricity usage ([EWG](https://www.ewg.org/news-insights/news-release/2026/08/california-legislature-approves-bill-easing-access-clean))
- Systems capped at **1,200W** (enough to power fridges, lights, Wi-Fi routers, and AC units) ([Solar Power World](https://www.solarpowerworldonline.com/2026/08/california-sends-balcony-solar-bill-to-governor-for-signature/))
- 11 other states have already passed similar laws, with California trailing Utah, Colorado, and others ([Climate-Colored Goggles](https://www.climatecoloredgoggles.com/p/california-balcony-solar))

**What remains:** Utilities successfully lobbied for a January 1, 2030 sunset clause requiring legislative reauthorization. This is a [PG&E priority](https://www.climatecoloredgoggles.com/p/california-balcony-solar) that sets up another fight in four years. The interconnection exemption will expire unless lawmakers extend it, giving utilities another chance to claw back control over distributed energy that threatens their monopoly model.

# UK makes plug-in solar legal nationwide, opening path for 800W balcony systems

**The Bottom Line:** Starting August 27, residents across England, Wales, and Scotland can legally install plug-in solar systems up to 800W without certified installers or planning permission, following amendments to UK plug and socket regulations and electrical safety rules. The systems cover up to 20% of average household electricity use and save families up to **£110** ($150) per year, according to [the UK government](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/plug-in-solar-panels-now-legal-in-great-britain). Major retailers including Argos, Screwfix, Amazon, and Currys are stocking systems, with Screwfix planning availability this fall.

**The Story:** Graham Biggart, managing director of catalogue retailer Argos, confirmed the company is "really pleased to now offer plug-in solar panels" to customers. Screwfix told pv magazine it plans to offer systems to customers this fall. The retail rollout matters because these aren't niche products requiring specialty suppliers. They're going mainstream through the same channels where UK households buy garden furniture and power tools.

**By the numbers:**

- Systems up to **800W** now legal without certified installation, covering up to 20% of average home electricity use ([UK government](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/plug-in-solar-panels-now-legal-in-great-britain))
- Households save up to **£110** per year from single plug-in array ([UK government](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/plug-in-solar-panels-now-legal-in-great-britain))
- 77% of UK households lack air conditioning as climate heats up, making accessible solar crucial for cooling power ([Energy Saving Trust](https://energysavingtrust.org.uk/advice/plug-in-solar-panels/))
- Regulation changes remove planning permission requirements in England; Scotland and Wales set own rules ([pv magazine](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/08/26/plug-in-solar-panels-now-legal-in-the-uk/))

**What remains:** Plug-in battery storage remains excluded from the regulatory approval pending a full safety review by the Energy Storage Association. Households cannot yet pair solar with storage to maximize value. Current UK battery regulations require outdoor installations and certified wiring checks. Standards for plug-in batteries and their impact on household circuits are still under investigation.

# Georgia Regulators Approve Secret 3.2 GW OpenAI Deal With No Public Vote

**The Bottom Line:** The Georgia Public Service Commission staff [approved a contract](https://psc.ga.gov/search/facts-document/?documentId=228126) between Georgia Power and OpenAI for a **3.2 GW** hyperscale data center in Effingham County without a direct vote from the commission's elected members. The deal went through after what Sierra Club and Southern Alliance for Clean Energy call a "tumultuous and secretive process" that bypassed public accountability for one of the largest utility contracts in state history.

**The Story:** Georgia Power and PSC staff co-released a letter designed to calm public concerns about whether the data center will raise everyone's electric bills. Instead, it left key questions unanswered: how is Georgia Power calculating the cost to serve these data centers, and what protections exist if the data center leaves or uses less power than contracted? Maggie Shober, Research Director at Southern Alliance for Clean Energy, notes that "it is up to the Commission itself to update this process to provide transparency and accountability to the public."

**By the numbers:**

- Georgia Power's contract covers **3.2 GW** of capacity for OpenAI's planned data center in Effingham County
- PSC staff approved the deal without a direct vote from the five elected commissioners
- Key cost calculations and ratepayer protections remain undisclosed in the joint statement
- This follows a pattern of utilities prioritizing data center deals nationwide, often at ratepayer expense

**What remains:** Elected commissioners at the PSC have no formal authority to weigh in on future large load contracts under the current process. Without updated rules requiring commission votes and public review, Georgia Power can continue negotiating billion-dollar deals behind closed doors while ratepayers absorb the risk.

# Solar kept Europe's lights on while nuclear and coal shut down in deadly heat

**The Bottom Line:** Solar supplied 25% of EU electricity during June and July's record-breaking heatwaves. These were the exact conditions that forced **11 GW** of French nuclear offline when rivers became too hot for cooling, dropped hydropower to decade lows, and cut **1.3 GW** from Polish coal plants as the Vistula dried up. Solar generation ran 17% higher than normal summer output in France and Hungary, keeping grids stable during peak demand while the 'reliable baseload' power sources went dark.

**The Story:** On July 12, with temperatures above 40°C across Western Europe, French nuclear operator EDF marked 11 gigawatts of capacity offline due to "environmental issues." River water was too warm to legally discharge back into waterways without killing aquatic life. At the same moment, solar panels across France were pumping out 17% more electricity than typical summer days, directly overlapping with afternoon air conditioning demand that had pushed Italian power consumption up 28% from the previous week. The irony was bitter: infrastructure designed as "always-on" baseload couldn't handle the heat, while the supposedly "intermittent" renewable showed up exactly when people needed it most.

**By the numbers:**

- Solar provided 25% of EU electricity in both June and July 2026 (the first time solar has supplied a quarter of European power, according to [Ember Energy](https://ember-energy.org/latest-insights/solar-helps-europes-grid-withstand-extreme-heat/))
- France lost up to **11 GW** (18%) of nuclear capacity to thermal shutdowns as rivers became too warm for cooling discharge
- UK gas plants reduced output by **2.5 GW** combined due to heat-reduced cooling effectiveness
- Poland's coal plants at Kozienice and Połaniec cut **1.3 GW** as the Vistula River dropped to critically low levels
- EU hydropower hit its lowest July level in a decade as drought persisted across the continent
- Evening electricity prices spiked to **€900/MWh** in Hungary and **€313/MWh** in France (the highest since the 2022-23 winter gas crisis, according to [Copernicus Climate Change Service](https://climate.copernicus.eu/copernicus-record-heatwave-brings-hottest-june-western-europe-during-second-warmest-june-globally))

**What remains:** Europe now needs batteries to shift daytime solar abundance into evening hours when cooling demand persists but solar stops producing. Evening price spikes prove the battery storage gap is now the critical infrastructure need, not because solar fails, but because legacy thermal infrastructure designed for stable climate can't handle the heat that's already here.
