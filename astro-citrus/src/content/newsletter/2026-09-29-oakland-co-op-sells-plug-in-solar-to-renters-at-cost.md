# Oakland co-op sells plug-in solar to renters at cost

**The Bottom Line:** [Bright Saver](https://www.brightsaver.org/), an Oakland-based nonprofit, sells plug-in balcony solar panels to renters at actual cost through a member co-op model. Members pay **$29** annually to access **180W** systems at **$285.30**, exactly what Bright Saver pays, with no markup. No landlord permission required. Panels plug into standard outdoor outlets.

**The Story:** The co-op's [second crowdfunding campaign](https://solarbuildermag.com/residential-solar/bright-saver-launches-second-balcony-solar-crowdfunding-effort/) follows its June 2025 offering, which hit **248%** of its funding goal and closed early. "We've already shown that people want this," says co-CEO Cora Stryker. "Now the opportunity is to scale it." The model turns membership into collective bargaining power. Bulk purchasing eliminates distributor markup, and growing membership signals demand to manufacturers and policymakers.

**By the numbers:**
- Bright Saver sells **180W** plug-in solar systems at **$285.30** with **$29** annual membership, [no markup over wholesale cost](https://solarbuildermag.com/residential-solar/bright-saver-launches-second-balcony-solar-crowdfunding-effort/)
- **44 million** U.S. renter households typically excluded from rooftop solar programs
- **9 states** changed laws to allow plug-in solar; **35 states** introduced legislation in 2026
- Plans to expand same at-cost model to batteries, heat pumps, and energy financing

**What remains:** Bright Saver has proven demand among early adopters, but reaching millions of renters requires scaling beyond direct crowdfunding campaigns and Oakland's climate-conscious base. The nonprofit needs consistent supply chains and broader state-level regulatory wins to make the model work nationwide.

# Albuquerque builds 6 MW of solar it won't own for 25 years

**The Bottom Line:** Albuquerque is installing about **6 MW** of solar across eight municipal facilities, including an animal shelter, pools, and the city airport. But a private company will own the infrastructure for 25 years while the city makes monthly payments. [Sustainability Partners](https://www.sustainability.partners/) provides capital and maintains the systems; Albuquerque avoids upfront costs but won't disclose total payment amounts, making it impossible to compare against municipal bonds that would give the city public ownership from day one.

**The Story:** At the Eastside Animal Shelter and Highland Pool, crews broke ground last week on rooftop and carport solar installations. The city will use the power immediately, paying monthly fees to a private company that retains ownership. Mayor Tim Keller framed it as fiscal pragmatism: "We're using every tool available to get more clean energy on the ground without putting the entire cost of building and maintaining these systems on taxpayers." But taxpayers are paying. The question is whether they're also building equity, or just renting.

**By the numbers:**
- Albuquerque is deploying **6 MW** of solar generation across eight city facilities, targeting 100% renewable municipal operations ([Solar Power World](https://www.solarpowerworldonline.com/2026/09/eight-solar-projects-should-bring-albuquerque-to-100-renewable-energy/))
- Facilities include the animal shelter, Highland Pool, West Mesa Aquatic Center, Duke City BMX Park, Isotopes Park, transit facility, 911 call center, and Albuquerque International Sunport
- The Sunport installation adds **10 EV chargers** and battery storage for critical electrical backup
- Construction phases through **2027**; private company retains ownership for **25 years**
- City has not disclosed total monthly payment obligations, preventing comparison to municipal bond financing costs

**What remains:** The core question goes unanswered: who should profit from renewable generation sited on public property? Without disclosed payment totals, residents can't evaluate whether this model serves them better than public ownership through municipal bonds, a financing tool cities use routinely for infrastructure they'll own outright.

# Fossil fuel chaos makes EVs nine times cheaper in UK

**The Bottom Line:** Electric vehicles now cost 2.3p per mile to drive in the UK using off-peak charging, compared to 21p per mile for diesel. That's a ninefold difference driven by oil price spikes from the Iran war and attacks on Russian refineries. UK drivers save £80 per tank equivalent by charging at home instead of filling up with petrol, according to [Carbon Brief analysis](https://www.carbonbrief.org/analysis-evs-are-now-nine-times-cheaper-than-petrol-or-diesel-to-drive-in-the-uk) of government fuel data.

**The Story:** Diesel hit £1.96 per liter in September and is heading past £2 for the first time as Russia's refineries face strikes every three days and oil tops $100 per barrel. Even at standard household electricity rates (not special EV tariffs), driving electric costs 7p per mile, still three times cheaper than petrol.

**By the numbers:**
- UK diesel prices jumped **38%** since February to **£1.96/liter**; petrol up **31%** to **£1.72/liter** ([UK DESNZ data](https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/weekly-road-fuel-prices))
- Off-peak EV charging costs **2.3p/mile** vs **21p/mile** for diesel ([Zapmap charging index](https://www.zapmap.com/ev-stats/charging-price-index))
- EV drivers save **£1,200/year** in fuel costs compared to petrol drivers
- Battery EVs now **30%** of new UK car sales in August 2026, up from 26.5% in 2025 ([SMMT registration data](https://www.smmt.co.uk/vehicle-data/electric-vehicle-registrations/))
- But only **6%** of UK's 35 million cars are fully electric

**What remains:** Petrol cars still make up 55% of UK vehicles despite being nine times more expensive to fuel. Upfront EV costs, limited public charging in some areas, and the lack of home charging access for renters create barriers that price advantage alone hasn't solved.

# Germany's solar boom meets policy cliff

**The Bottom Line:** German solar installers are having their best year in decades with full order books through 2026, but proposed federal legislation limiting rooftop solar grid feed-in to 50% is already causing bankruptcies and financing freezes before the law even passes. Project developer [Enerparc and inverter manufacturer RCT Power](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/09/18/regulatory-uncertainty-threatens-to-slow-german-pv-deployment-in-2027/) have collapsed as banks pull solar loans, citing regulatory uncertainty. The proposed cap lacks transitional measures, triggering a market rush as customers race to commission systems under current rules.

**The Story:** Ask installers how business is going and most say excellent. Ask banks and project developers the same question and they describe financing markets freezing. Installation companies have waitlists extending into next year while major solar firms announce insolvency. Industry veterans recognize this whiplash from 2012-2014, when subsidy cuts championed by Environment Minister Peter Altmaier killed more than half of Germany's solar companies after three strong years, a collapse known as the "Altmaier effect."

**By the numbers:**

- **50%** grid feed-in cap proposed for new residential solar systems, [with no transition period or grandfathering provisions](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/09/18/regulatory-uncertainty-threatens-to-slow-german-pv-deployment-in-2027/)
- **Over 50%** of German solar companies went bankrupt during the 2012-2014 Altmaier subsidy cuts
- **Two** major German solar companies (Enerparc, RCT Power) declared bankruptcy in September 2026 as financing dried up
- Banks tightening loan terms and rejecting new solar financing requests due to regulatory uncertainty, per industry reports

**What remains:** The bills haven't passed the Bundestag or Bundesrat yet, so transitional measures could still be added. But the pattern is clear: fossil fuel utilities are using regulatory capture to kill decentralized solar that cuts into their profits, just as they did in 2012.
