# Solar carries Europe through deadly heatwaves as nuclear and hydro collapse

**The Bottom Line:** Solar supplied a record 25% of EU electricity in June and July 2026 as successive heatwaves pushed temperatures above 40°C, killed hundreds, and forced thermal plants offline across the continent. While air conditioning demand spiked up to 28% in Italy and Hungary, [solar generation ran 17% higher](https://ember-energy.org/latest-insights/solar-helps-europes-grid-withstand-extreme-heat/) than typical summer days in France and Hungary, keeping grids stable during daylight hours when other power sources couldn't deliver. This wasn't a pilot project. This was solar doing the work during the exact conditions that broke legacy infrastructure.

**The Story:** On July 12, France had 11 GW of nuclear capacity offline because river water was too hot to safely discharge back into waterways without killing aquatic life. Hungary and Romania's Danube-cooled reactors, which normally provide 40% and 15% of their countries' electricity, hit record low water levels that threatened complete shutdowns. Poland's Enea cut 1.3 GW from its Kozienice and Połaniec coal plants when the Vistula River couldn't supply enough cooling water. Solar panels across the EU generated 55 TWh in July alone, their highest monthly output on record.

**By the numbers:**

- Daily electricity demand increased up to 28% in Italy, 23% in Hungary, 14% in France, and 13% in Spain during heatwave periods compared to pre-heatwave weeks, according to [Ember's analysis](https://ember-energy.org/latest-insights/solar-helps-europes-grid-withstand-extreme-heat/)
- France had a maximum of 29 GW (43%) of nuclear capacity unavailable on July 12, with at least 11 GW marked as forced outages or environmental issues
- EU hydropower hit its lowest July level in a decade, with seven of eight largest producers below five-year averages
- Evening electricity prices spiked to €900/MWh in Hungary on June 30 and €313/MWh in France on June 24, highest since the 2022 gas crisis

**What remains:** The evening price spikes expose Europe's battery storage gap. Solar kept daytime prices stable, but when the sun set and cooling demand remained high, grids relied on expensive gas generation, pushing prices to crisis levels. Europe needs utility-scale batteries to shift cheap solar power into evening hours. That's the infrastructure that prevents €900/MWh emergencies from becoming routine as heatwaves intensify.

# Next-gen geothermal just proved itself: Sage's Texas plant hits 120 days commercial operation

**The Bottom Line:** [Sage Geosystems](https://www.canarymedia.com/articles/geothermal/sage-geosystems-next-gen-geothermal-online) is producing power from its first enhanced geothermal plant near San Antonio: a 3 MW pilot that's been running commercially for over 120 days with less than 10% water loss. It's only the third enhanced geothermal system to connect to the US grid, and it directly feeds into Sage's deal to develop 150 MW of geothermal power for Meta data centers. Unlike traditional geothermal that requires rare volcanic geology, this system drills into hot dry rock, fractures it, and uses pressure cycling to drive turbines. That potentially unlocks baseload clean power anywhere with hot rocks underground.

**The Story:** Sage's approach solves geothermal's geography problem. Traditional geothermal plants tap into naturally occurring hot water reservoirs, limiting deployment to places like Iceland or California's geysers. Enhanced geothermal systems drill 4 to 8 miles down into hot rock, fracture it to create pathways, then pump water through to extract heat. Sage's twist: they use the pressure itself to generate electricity, eliminating the need for surface turbines and cutting water use to nearly zero. The 120-day run proves the system can operate commercially, not just in a lab.

**By the numbers:**
- 120+ days of continuous commercial operation at Sage's San Antonio pilot plant ([Canary Media](https://www.canarymedia.com/articles/geothermal/sage-geosystems-next-gen-geothermal-online))
- Less than 10% water loss during operation, proving the closed-loop system works at scale
- 3 MW capacity at the pilot plant, enough to power roughly 2,000 homes
- 150 MW contracted capacity for Meta data centers, marking one of the largest next-gen geothermal deals in the U.S.
- Only the third enhanced geothermal system to connect to the US grid, following projects in Nevada and Utah

**What remains:** Scaling from a 3 MW pilot to 150 MW of commercial capacity is a 50x jump that Sage hasn't yet demonstrated. The timeline and cost for that buildout will determine whether enhanced geothermal can compete with solar-plus-storage for baseload power.

# When the fuel chain fails: Orange County's $108M hydrogen bus bet collapses 95%

**The Bottom Line:** The [Orange County Transportation Authority](https://www.octa.net/)'s hydrogen bus fleet dropped from 270,000 miles in 2024 to just 14,000 miles in 2025, a 95% operational collapse, after its Santa Ana fueling station became unusable when OCTA and Air Products failed to reach a new supply agreement. Despite this failure, OCTA is spending another $27.6 million on hydrogen infrastructure at Garden Grove while expanding its fuel-cell bus fleet from 10 to 50 buses.

**The Story:** Air Products removed its leased 18,000-gallon liquid-hydrogen tank and vaporizers in January 2026 after the commercial agreement fell through. The buses themselves still had years of service life. They just couldn't get fuel. OCTA scrambled to send buses to a commercial Shell station near Long Beach and arranged temporary mobile fueling. Meanwhile, OCTA's battery-electric buses, operating on the same routes with the same agency, reported zero lost deployments because of charging infrastructure unavailability.

**By the numbers:**
- OCTA has spent $108 million total on hydrogen refueling infrastructure versus $6 million for battery-electric charging, [according to TFIE Strategy Briefing](https://briefing.tfie.io/p/octas-hydrogen-bus-expansion-is-really)
- Hydrogen bus operations collapsed from 270,462 miles in 2024 to 14,232 miles in 2025, a 95% decline
- The failed Santa Ana station was sized for 40 to 50 buses per day when it opened in 2020, only six years before becoming unusable
- OCTA's new [$27.6 million Garden Grove station contract](https://octa.legistar.com/View.ashx?GUID=5A90B734-E016-4C97-8EF1-4CD47D5C9E45&ID=15451907&M=F) bundles construction, fuel supply, operations, maintenance and training because hydrogen requires an entire fuel pathway, not just a dispenser
- Battery-bus charging infrastructure reported 80% equipment availability but zero lost bus deployments, compared to near-total hydrogen fleet grounding

**What remains:** OCTA is doubling down with 40 more hydrogen buses instead of following Aberdeen's lead and writing off the failed experiment. The agency now plans a $19 million replacement at Santa Ana plus the new Garden Grove station, building redundancy into a fuel system that costs 18 times more than battery charging and has already failed once. The buses work fine when they can get fuel. The fuel chain is what keeps breaking.

# Florida district bypasses grid with 375 solar streetlights, pays monthly fee instead

**The Bottom Line:** The [Sun 'N Lake Improvement District](https://www.sunlakewater.com/) in Florida deployed 375 off-grid solar streetlights across public roads through an infrastructure-as-a-service model: no capital outlay, no utility coordination, no trenching, no debt issuance. The district pays a monthly service fee to [Sustainability Partners](https://www.sustainabilitypartners.com/), which owns and maintains the systems. Each standalone pole combines solar panel, lithium battery, and LED fixture.

**The Story:** District Manager Raymond Bossert needed to improve nighttime safety but faced the usual obstacles: upfront capital costs, utility coordination delays, and expensive trenching to extend grid connections. By contracting the lights as a service, the district turned a capital project into an operational expense. The lights stay on during grid outages, critical for Florida's hurricane season.

**By the numbers:**

- 375 solar streetlights deployed across the district's jurisdiction, per [pv magazine](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2026/08/20/florida-district-deploys-375-solar-streetlights-via-infrastructure-as-a-service-model/)
- District pays monthly usage fee based on illumination hours; Sustainability Partners covers all capital, installation, and maintenance costs
- Each system includes 100 W bifacial monocrystalline panel, 12.8 V lithium iron phosphate battery, and LED array providing up to 15 hours continuous illumination
- Miller Electric Company handled procurement and installation across multi-phase rollout

**What remains:** The infrastructure-as-a-service model accelerates deployment but raises questions about long-term public ownership of basic services. When a private company owns the lights illuminating public streets, communities trade immediate access for ongoing financial obligation and reduced local control.
