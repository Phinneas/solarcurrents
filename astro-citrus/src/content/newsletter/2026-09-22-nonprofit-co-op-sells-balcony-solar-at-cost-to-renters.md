# Nonprofit co-op sells balcony solar at cost to renters

**The Bottom Line:** Oakland-based nonprofit [Bright Saver](https://www.brightsaver.org/) launched its second crowdfunding campaign to scale a member co-op that sells plug-in solar kits at cost. The organization charges a $29 annual membership fee, then sells 180W balcony systems for $285.30 with no markup. After reaching 248% of its first funding target in June 2025, Bright Saver plans to apply the same bulk-buying model to batteries, heat pumps, and energy financing.

**The Story:** Bright Saver's co-CEO Cora Stryker says collective buying power works for clean energy hardware. "We've already shown that people want this," [Stryker told Solar Builder](https://solarbuildermag.com/residential-solar/bright-saver-launches-second-balcony-solar-crowdfunding-effort/). "Now the opportunity is to scale it." The nonprofit purchases panels in bulk, then passes savings directly to members rather than adding retail markup. It's infrastructure built around renters who'll never own a roof.

**By the numbers:**
- $285.30 per 180W plug-in solar system at cost, plus $29 annual membership (Bright Saver)
- 248% of original crowdfunding target reached in June 2025, [campaign closed early](https://solarbuildermag.com/residential-solar/bright-saver-launches-second-balcony-solar-crowdfunding-effort/) (Solar Builder)
- 35 states introduced balcony solar legislation, 9 states changed laws to legalize plug-in systems (Bright Saver)
- 44 million U.S. renter households now have potential legal access to balcony solar where legislation passed

**What remains:** Most states still haven't legalized balcony solar, and the co-op model depends on reaching enough members to maintain bulk purchasing power. Bright Saver's expansion to other technologies faces the same challenge: scaling membership fast enough to negotiate manufacturer pricing.

# Albuquerque deploys 6 MW of solar it won't own for 25 years

**The Bottom Line:** Albuquerque broke ground on approximately 6 MW of solar across eight municipal facilities (animal shelter, pools, transit center, airport, 911 center) to close the gap to 100% renewable municipal energy. [Sustainability Partners](https://www.sustainability.partners/), a Public Benefit Company, owns the infrastructure for 25 years while the city makes monthly usage payments. Mayor Tim Keller says this uses "every tool available" to deploy clean energy without upfront capital costs.

**The Story:** Construction started last week at the [Eastside Animal Shelter and Highland Pool](https://www.solarpowerworldonline.com/2026/09/eight-solar-projects-should-bring-albuquerque-to-100-renewable-energy/), with rooftop arrays and solar carports planned through 2027. At the Sunport airport installation, the project adds 10 EV chargers and battery storage to support critical electrical needs. Energy Systems Group designed the installations; Sustainability Partners finances and maintains them.

**By the numbers:**
- ~6 MW of solar generation across eight city facilities, phased through 2027
- 25 years of private ownership before potential city acquisition
- 10 EV chargers plus battery storage at Albuquerque International Sunport
- Albuquerque reaches 100% renewable energy for municipal operations with this deployment

**What remains:** The total 25-year cost to taxpayers isn't disclosed. The city treats solar like a utility bill rather than public infrastructure it owns. A model that deploys clean energy now but raises questions about who profits from publicly sited renewable generation and whether municipal bond financing would serve ratepayers better than private lease payments.

# Germany threatens to repeat its solar industry collapse: the 'Reiche effect'

**The Bottom Line:** Germany's solar sector is booming in 2026, but proposed regulations threaten a 2027 market collapse that could mirror the 2012-2014 "Altmaier effect," when subsidy cuts killed half the country's solar companies. [Minister Katherina Reiche's government](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/09/18/regulatory-uncertainty-threatens-to-slow-german-pv-deployment-in-2027/) is pushing a 50% cap on grid feed-in with no transitional measures, triggering financing freezes and insolvencies even as installation demand remains strong. Banks are tightening loan terms, major developers like Enerparc have filed for insolvency, and manufacturers like RCT Power are shuttering operations.

**The Story:** Installation companies report 2026 as their best year in a long time, with order books full through December as customers rush to commission systems under current regulations. But behind the demand surge, project developers can't secure financing for new builds. Banks are pulling back because the proposed law removes the Renewable Energy Sources Act's payment guarantees that made solar projects bankable. Installers are selling the rush by warning customers that systems commissioned after the law passes may never be economically viable.

**By the numbers:**
- Germany's solar industry lost more than half its companies between 2012-2014 after Environment Minister Peter Altmaier pushed through [drastic subsidy cuts](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/09/18/regulatory-uncertainty-threatens-to-slow-german-pv-deployment-in-2027/)
- The proposed legislation imposes a 50% cap on grid feed-in for new systems, with no transitional provisions for projects already in development
- Major solar companies including project developer Enerparc and inverter manufacturer RCT Power have filed for insolvency in 2026
- Installation companies report full order books through year-end, but banks are tightening terms on existing loans and rejecting new financing requests

**What remains:** This isn't technical policy. It's about protecting fossil fuel utilities from the "inconvenient decentralization" that solar creates. If the 50% feed-in cap passes unchanged, only companies that can pivot to battery-heavy or off-grid business models will survive the 2027 contraction.

# When lowest-bid procurement corrodes

**The Bottom Line:** A two-year-old solar park on Portugal's Atlantic coast is rusting because the [EPC contractor](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/09/09/fighting-coastal-corrosion/) used standard galvanized bolts instead of marine-grade stainless steel fasteners to win the lowest-bid contract. The contractor is now paying warranty claims to replace hardware that should have lasted 25 years. This is how lowest-bid procurement culture undermines solar's 25-year design life: prioritize upfront cost, gamble that corrosion won't surface until after warranty expires, and pass replacement costs to someone else when it does.

**The Story:** The project owner was walking his dog when he noticed rust on bolts at his coastal installation, only two years after commissioning. He invoked the German EPC's workmanship guarantee. The contractor is replacing the failed fasteners under warranty, but the damage reveals a procurement model built on corner-cutting rather than lifecycle performance.

**By the numbers:**
- Marine-grade stainless steel fasteners cost 3-5% more than standard galvanized bolts but eliminate corrosion in coastal environments ([pv magazine](https://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/09/09/fighting-coastal-corrosion/))
- Standard solar project warranties run 2-5 years for workmanship, while equipment is designed for 25-year lifetimes
- Salt spray accelerates corrosion in coastal zones within 500 meters of the ocean, requiring higher-grade materials

**What remains:** Industry procurement practices still reward lowest upfront cost over lifecycle durability. Until contracts account for replacement expenses and performance degradation, EPCs will continue gambling with cheap materials, and asset owners will pay for it after warranties expire.
