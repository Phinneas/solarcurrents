Panels on the roof does not mean panels are legally allowed to run. Between "installed" and "turned on," most residential solar systems sit inside a 10-to-20-week utility review window, and in several Southeast and Mid-Atlantic territories that window has stretched to 45–75 business days for the utility's piece alone.¹ ² That gap is the interconnection agreement, and it's the step almost no sales conversation walks you through before you sign.

**Interconnection agreement, defined:** it's a formal contract between you (or your installer, on your behalf) and your electric utility that permits your solar system to legally connect to and operate in parallel with the grid.³ It covers your system's exact specifications, the safety equipment that protects utility line workers when your inverter is exporting power, and who's responsible for what if something goes wrong. Typical documents run 12–15 pages.⁴ Nothing about it is optional, and nothing about it happens automatically when your installer finishes wiring the system.

## Why This Step Exists at All

Every grid-tied solar system is a power source the utility didn't build and doesn't directly control. Before they'll let it export electricity onto their lines, they need to confirm three things: your inverter will shut off automatically if utility power goes down (anti-islanding, so line workers aren't electrocuted by your system feeding a "dead" line), your local circuit and transformer can actually absorb the extra generation without tripping protective equipment, and your equipment is certified to the relevant standards (UL 1741 for the inverter, NEC Article 705 for the wiring).⁵ The agreement is the utility's paper trail that all three are true for your specific address and equipment.

## The Timeline, Broken Down

"Contract-to-PTO" (Permission to Operate) is the full span from signing your solar contract to the utility flipping the switch that lets you actually generate. National Grid's published data puts that at roughly three to five months — a 12-to-20-week average — for residential systems in its territory in 2026.¹ That's the whole journey: design, permitting, installation, inspection, and interconnection combined.

The interconnection piece specifically — the utility's own review, separate from your local building permit — breaks down roughly like this for a typical residential system with no complications:

| Stage | Typical duration | What's happening |
|---|---|---|
| Application submission | 1–3 days | Installer submits system design, equipment specs, one-line diagram |
| Utility technical review | 2–4 weeks (up to 45–75 business days in some Southeast/Mid-Atlantic territories) | Utility checks circuit capacity, confirms anti-islanding and equipment listings |
| Local AHJ plan review + inspection | 2–4 weeks | Building/electrical/fire department reviews plans, then inspects after install² |
| Utility final approval + net meter install | 1–3 weeks | Utility installs a bidirectional meter, issues PTO |

Add them up with normal handoff delays between steps and you land in the same 10-to-20-week range most utilities now publish for residential PTO.⁶ Commercial and larger systems run longer: 30–60 days for initial review, and another 60–120 days on top of that if the utility requires a full system-impact study.⁷

## What Actually Causes the Delays

The honest answer is rarely "the utility is slow for no reason." Three things drive most of the timeline:

**Incomplete or incorrect applications.** Missing equipment documentation, mismatched one-line diagrams, or a circuit that needs extra engineering review because of existing load on that transformer are the most common causes of a restudy — and a restudy resets the clock.⁸ This is squarely on the installer's side of the process, and it's worth asking directly whether they use a pre-application or pre-screening review with your utility before formal submission; Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory data shows projects that do reach final approval 40–60% faster than those that skip it.⁹

**Grid capacity on your specific circuit.** If your transformer or circuit segment is already carrying a lot of distributed solar, the utility may need to run a supplemental study before approving another system on it. This is a real, growing issue in some markets — but be skeptical of any article that cites the "interconnection queue crisis" headline number (over 2,000 GW of generation capacity waiting in U.S. interconnection queues) as the reason your home system is delayed.¹⁰ That figure is almost entirely utility-scale, transmission-connected projects — wind farms, solar farms, battery plants — waiting in a completely different queue, under FERC's jurisdiction, than the distribution-level agreement a homeowner signs with their local utility under state Public Utility Commission rules.¹¹ ¹² Don't let a sales rep or a blog post explain your five-week wait with a statistic about a different process.

**Plain understaffing.** Interconnection departments at many utilities are handling application volumes that have grown much faster than their review staff. This is the least glamorous cause and the most common one.

## What You Can Actually Do While Waiting

Not much of this is in your direct control, but a few things are:

- **Ask your installer for the application confirmation number and utility portal access**, if your utility offers one, so you can check status yourself instead of waiting on installer updates.
- **Confirm the application was submitted complete the first time.** If your installer can't tell you whether pre-application review was used or whether the utility has requested any follow-up documents, that's worth pushing on.
- **Understand your financial exposure during the wait.** If you financed the system with a loan or signed a lease/PPA, payments often start on installation, not on PTO — meaning you can be paying for a system that isn't legally allowed to run yet. Read your contract's payment-start clause before you sign, not after.
- **Escalate through your state's utility regulator if the wait is well outside published norms.** Most state Public Utility Commissions (searchable through NARUC) publish average interconnection timelines by utility, so you can check whether your wait is normal or an outlier worth a formal complaint.

## The Bottom Line

The interconnection agreement isn't a formality your installer handles invisibly — it's a multi-week-to-multi-month utility review with real failure points, and it's the single biggest reason a fully installed system can sit dark for weeks or months. Ask your installer, before you sign anything, what your specific utility's average interconnection timeline is and whether they use pre-application review. That's not a market-average number from this piece; it's the number for your address, and it's the one that actually determines when your system turns on.
