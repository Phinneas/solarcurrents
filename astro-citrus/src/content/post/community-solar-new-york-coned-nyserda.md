New York is the community solar market that actually works — over 7.3 GW of distributed solar installed with another 2.8 GW in the pipeline, the most of any state. If you pay a ConEd, National Grid, NYSEG, RG&E, Central Hudson, or Orange & Rockland electric bill, you can almost certainly subscribe to a solar farm and save real money. That's the honest headline, and it's better than most states can offer.

But "10% off your electric bill" — the number on every provider's landing page — is not quite what you'll experience, and the details differ meaningfully between ConEd territory and the rest of the state. Here's the math the sign-up pages simplify away.

## How the money actually flows

New York community solar runs on Community Distributed Generation (CDG) rules. You subscribe to a share of an offsite solar project (5 MW or smaller). Your share's output gets valued each month under the state's VDER "Value Stack" — a formula that prices the energy based on when and where it hit the grid, plus capacity, environmental, and locational components — and lands on your utility bill as a dollar credit.

Then the provider bills you for those credits at a discount. Nexamp's own example is the clearest: if your credits are worth $100, your utility bill drops by $100 and Nexamp invoices you $90. Your saving is the $10 spread — 10% *of the credit value*, not 10% of your total bill. If your bill includes delivery charges your credits don't fully offset, your effective whole-bill savings run lower than the advertised number.

The standard offer across New York in 2026 is a 10% discount, with 20% for income-qualified (LMI) households. Some states get 15% from the same providers; New York's 10% reflects a maturing market where the juiciest incentive tranches are gone — more on that below.

## One bill or two — check before you sign

New York requires utilities to offer "net crediting," which consolidates everything into your utility bill: credits appear, the subscription charge appears, one statement, done. Utilities take a 1% administrative fee for this. It's the arrangement to prefer — you can verify your savings on a single page.

But not every subscription works this way anymore. Nexamp moved to dual billing for some New York projects in 2026: credits on your utility bill, subscription invoice arriving separately. The discount is the same, but you now have to reconcile two documents to confirm you're actually ahead, and autopay on a second account is where subscribers stop paying attention. Ask explicitly: "Will my subscription charge appear on my utility bill or as a separate invoice?"

## ConEd territory: better credits, tighter supply

If you're a ConEd customer (NYC and most of Westchester), two things cut in opposite directions.

Credits are worth more. ConEd's Value Stack rates are the highest in the state — the environmental component alone is about 3.1¢/kWh in 2026, and ConEd projects earned a Community Credit adder of 12¢/kWh, locked for 25 years, on top of base value. Dense-grid locations add locational value upstate projects can't match.

Supply is tighter. That 12¢ Community Credit tranche is fully subscribed — 350 of 350 MW, closed. The state's build target for ConEd territory (450 MW incremental, roughly 1 GW total by 2030) is a fraction of what's planned upstate, because there's simply less room for 5 MW arrays in the five boroughs. The practical consequence: waitlists. Providers will happily take your sign-up today and assign you to a farm when a spot opens — Nexamp says as much on its New York page. Signing up costs nothing, but "you're enrolled" and "you're receiving credits" can be months apart. Ask which project you're being assigned to and when it energizes.

Also specific to ConEd: the NYCHA rooftop program (marketed as "Community Power") gives participating residents 20% of the credit value from panels on housing authority roofs — about $120 a year. It's free money if you're eligible, but note the structure: you keep 20%, and 80% funds the program. That's a community-benefit design, not a market subscription.

## Upstate: more supply, lower credit values

National Grid, NYSEG, RG&E, and Central Hudson territories host most of the state's community solar — about 73% of new capacity is targeted upstate, where land is available. Farms have open capacity more often, so you're likelier to start earning credits within a billing cycle or three rather than sitting on a waitlist.

The credits themselves are worth less per kWh (lower locational and capacity values), but your discount percentage is the same, so as a subscriber you mostly don't care — the provider's revenue takes the hit, not your 10%. The subscriber experience upstate is usually simpler: more projects, faster assignment, same discount.

## Where NYSERDA fits (and where it doesn't)

NYSERDA doesn't sell subscriptions. NY-Sun pays *developers* upfront incentives per watt to build projects; the Inclusive Community Solar Adder and Solar Energy Equity Framework steer 40% of program benefits toward disadvantaged communities and LMI households. That's why the 20% LMI discount exists — providers pass through incentive value to hit equity targets.

Two things worth knowing in 2026: New York effectively hit its 10 GW distributed solar goal early, and NY-Sun's upfront incentives are phasing out — the PSC redirected $150 million of surplus into "Statewide Solar for All," a utility-administered program that applies solar bill credits directly to low-income customers' accounts with no subscription or sign-up at all. If you're on an affordability rate, check whether you're already receiving Solar for All credits before signing any subscription — stacking a subscription on top is allowed, but you should know what you're already getting.

NYSERDA's community solar page is also the right starting point for finding vetted providers, and their project map beats Googling "community solar near me," which surfaces whoever bought the ad.

## Co-ops, condos, and renters

Subscriptions attach to your individual utility account, not your building. If you're in a co-op or condo and pay your own ConEd bill, you can subscribe like any renter — no board approval needed, nothing installed. If your building master-meters electricity (you don't get your own ConEd bill), you can't subscribe individually; that's a board-level conversation about the building becoming a subscriber or an anchor. Buildings and businesses can take up to 40% of a project as "anchor" subscribers, so a motivated board has real options.

## The checklist before you enroll

New York's rules are consumer-friendly by national standards, but verify these five things: the discount rate and whether it's guaranteed for the contract's life (10% standard, 20% if income-qualified — take the LMI verification step if you might qualify); billing structure (net crediting on one bill, or a separate invoice); cancellation terms (typical NY contracts have no cancellation fee with 90 days' notice — confirm yours matches the norm before assuming); waitlist status (which farm, and when does it energize); and what happens to excess credits (in New York, unused credits bank on your utility account indefinitely — they don't expire, but they also never convert to cash).

The verdict: New York community solar is legitimate and the closest thing to free money in residential energy — a real discount, no equipment, no fees to join, and cancellation without penalty in standard contracts. The honest caveats are timing (waitlists in ConEd territory), the gap between "10% of credits" and what that means for your whole bill, and dual billing quietly replacing the single-bill experience at some providers. Read the four corners of the contract anyway. It's two pages. That's cheap insurance.
