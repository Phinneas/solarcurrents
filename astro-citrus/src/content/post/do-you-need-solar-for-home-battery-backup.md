A home battery that charges from the grid — no solar panels attached — costs $8,000 to $20,000 installed in 2026, with most homeowners landing around $13,000. Add solar panels to get there instead of skipping them, and the combined system runs $35,500 to $50,500 before incentives. If the only goal is keeping the lights on during an outage, solar is optional. If the goal is also cutting the monthly bill or eventually adding solar anyway, the math changes — and the rest of this piece walks through both paths with real numbers instead of a sales pitch.

## What "battery backup without solar" actually means

A standalone home battery — Tesla Powerwall 3, FranklinWH aPower, Enphase IQ Battery, Generac PWRcell — wires into the main electrical panel and charges from ordinary grid electricity, the same way a water heater or dryer draws power. An automatic transfer switch detects an outage and flips the home to battery power within milliseconds. No rooftop panels, no net metering agreement, no HOA solar approval required. The tradeoff: without solar recharging it during daylight, the battery is a finite reserve. Once it drains during a multi-day outage, it stays dead until utility power returns.

## Methodology: where these numbers come from

The installed price ranges below come from 2026 pricing published by EnergySage, Solar.com, and NuWatt for the four most-installed residential battery brands, cross-checked against solar-plus-storage totals reported by the same sources for the same year. Sizing figures use the standard formula — daily kWh usage ÷ depth of discharge ÷ days of autonomy — applied to typical U.S. household consumption of 25–35 kWh/day for a roughly 2,000-square-foot home.

| | Battery only (grid-charged) | Solar + battery |

| Installed cost (2026) | $8,000–$20,000 | $35,500–$50,500 |
| Recharges during an outage | No — fixed reserve until grid returns | Yes, during daylight |

## Sizing it correctly matters more than the brand

Two households buying "a battery" can need very different capacity. Essential-circuits-only backup — fridge, lights, wifi, a handful of outlets, no HVAC — needs roughly 10–15 kWh, enough for 24–36 hours on a single charge. Whole-house backup for a multi-day outage in an all-electric home with EV charging can require 40–60 kWh, often stacking two or three battery units. Chemistry changes the math again: lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4), used in nearly every current residential system, delivers roughly 95% usable capacity, versus roughly 50% for older lead-acid banks — meaning a lead-acid system needs close to double the nameplate capacity to deliver the same backup hours. Pull your last 12 months of utility bills and divide monthly kWh by 30 to get an honest daily-use number before sizing anything; installer estimates tend to round up.

## The tax credit nobody mentions until you ask

The Section 25D residential clean energy credit — the 30% credit that made batteries paired with solar meaningfully cheaper — expired for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025. A standalone battery bought in 2026 gets no federal credit at all, solar-paired or not. Some installer sites and quotes still reference the old 30% figure from posts written before the expiration; if a proposal leans on that credit to make the math work, ask directly when the system will be placed in service and get the current incentive stack in writing, because it no longer applies automatically. State and utility rebates — which vary widely and change yearly — are the only remaining lever in most markets, so check what's actually available in your state rather than trusting a number quoted online.

## The other reason to skip solar: rate arbitrage

Even without solar, a grid-charged battery can lower a bill if the local utility has a steep time-of-use spread — charging overnight at the off-peak rate and discharging during the expensive peak window instead of buying grid power then. At roughly a 20-cent-per-kWh peak/off-peak spread — the rough threshold where this starts to pencil out, covered in our [utility TOU rate spread breakdown](/posts/tou-rate-spread-battery-worth-it-map/) — a 10 kWh usable battery cycled daily saves about $2/day, or roughly $700/year, which closes a $10,000–$15,000 installed cost over 14-plus years before accounting for degradation and warranty-covered cycle limits (most residential batteries warranty 4,000–10,000 cycles, roughly 10–15 years of daily cycling). That's a real savings lever, but a slow one — it doesn't turn a standalone battery into a fast-payback investment the way self-consumed solar generation can. ## So: do you need solar to get the battery?

No. If outage protection is the actual goal, a standalone grid-charged battery does that job at less than half the installed cost of adding solar, with a simpler permitting process and no roof work involved. If a big time-of-use rate spread applies locally, or solar is already part of the longer-term plan, running the combined-system numbers now can avoid paying for a transfer switch and panel upgrade twice. But "get a battery so I'm ready for outages" and "go solar" are two different decisions with two different price tags — conflating them is how a $13,000 project turns into a $45,000 one without anyone deciding that's what they actually wanted.
