Yes — but not the way the solar industry usually means it. Rooftop panels, the 30% tax credit, net metering: that whole machine is built for people who own their roof. As a renter you can't sign a 25-year lease on someone else's shingles, and most "solar for renters" content quietly pivots to selling you a gadget.

Here's the actual decision, laid out by what you're trying to accomplish. There are five real paths, they solve different problems, and at least one of them almost certainly applies to you.

## First, the question that sorts everything: what's your goal?

"Going solar" as a renter means one of three things, and mixing them up is how people buy the wrong thing. Lowering your electric bill points you to community solar or balcony solar. Backup power when the grid fails points you to a power station, with or without panels. Power where there are no outlets — camping, van, job site — points you to portable panels. Nothing below requires your landlord's roof.

## Path 1: Community solar — the no-hardware option

If your state has a real program, this is the strongest bill-reduction play: subscribe to a share of an offsite solar farm, get credits on your utility bill, pay the provider slightly less than the credits are worth. No equipment, no installation, nothing to move when your lease ends. In strong markets like New York, the standard deal is a 10% discount on credit value (20% if income-qualified) with no cancellation fee — the details and traps are in our [New York guide](/posts/community-solar-new-york-coned-nyserda/). California is a different story: the honest answer there is that only income-qualified households in disadvantaged communities get a real discount, and we break down why in the [California community solar guide](/posts/community-solar-california-compare-plans-avoid-traps/). For everywhere else, our [community solar overview](/posts/community-solar-programs/) covers how the model works and what to check in any contract.

If your state has a program and you do nothing else from this page, do this one. It's the only path with zero upfront cost.

## Path 2: Balcony solar — the new one, where legal

Plug-in solar kits that hang on a balcony railing and feed power into an outlet are standard in Germany (over a million installed) and finally getting legal footing in the US. Utah went first with [HB 340](/posts/utah-hb-340-2025-explained/), and several states have followed with their own rules — the current map is in our [balcony solar laws by state hub](/posts/balcony-solar-laws-by-state/), with detail pages for [California](/posts/california-balcony-solar-law-2026), [Colorado](/posts/colorado-balcony-solar-law), and [New York](/posts/new-york-balcony-solar-law).

Honest math: a 400–800W kit offsets daytime plug loads — a few hundred kWh a year, roughly $50–150 depending on your rate. It will not zero out your bill. Legality depends on your state, your utility's interconnection rules, and — often forgotten — your lease and landlord. Read the [balcony solar overview](/posts/balcony-solar-panels/) before buying anything, and note the [safety considerations](/posts/plug-and-play-solar-panels/) around UL-listed inverters. Renting doesn't exempt you from any of it.

## Path 3: Window and patio solar — small, legal everywhere, small results

[Window solar panels](/posts/window-solar-panels/) and [patio setups](/posts/patio-solar-panels/) skirt the interconnection question entirely by charging batteries and devices instead of feeding your apartment's wiring. Nothing to permit, nothing your landlord can object to. The trade-off is scale: these are phone-and-laptop offsets, not bill-changers. Useful if your goal is genuinely small or you want to learn the technology cheaply; a disappointment if you were promised meaningful savings.

## Path 4: Portable power station + panels — backup power that moves with you

If your real worry is outages, the renter answer is a battery power station charged by portable panels — no transfer switch, no electrician, no landlord conversation. Sizing it honestly (what you can actually run, for how long) is where most buyers go wrong; start with our [solar generator sizing guide](/posts/solar-generator-sizing-calculator/) and the [emergency solar power breakdown](/posts/emergency-solar-power-system/). This gear is also the answer for camping and van setups, covered in our [renter-friendly setups roundup](/posts/best-solar-setups-renters/).

## Path 5: Talk to your landlord anyway

The long shot that occasionally pays off: buildings can host solar that benefits tenants. A landlord can install rooftop solar under a shared-meter arrangement, and in some states multifamily buildings can become community solar hosts or anchor subscribers — in New York, a master-metered building's board can subscribe the whole building even when individual tenants can't. If your building has a flat, sunny roof and an owner who answers email, our [5 types of solar without owning a home](/posts/5-types-of-solar-you-can-get-without-owning-a-home/) includes the building-level options worth forwarding.

## The bottom line

Match the path to the goal: community solar for the bill (if your state's program is real), balcony solar for the bill where legal (modest but yours), a power station for outages, portable panels for off-grid, and a landlord conversation if the building has potential. What renters should skip: anything marketed as "solar savings" that's actually a retail electricity contract in costume, and any hardware purchase before checking your state's rules and your lease. The good news is real: for the first time, a renter with a utility bill and a balcony has more than zero options — in the right state, several of them stack.
