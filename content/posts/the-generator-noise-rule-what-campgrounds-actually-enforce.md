Every campground has a "quiet hours" sign. Almost nobody reads the generator rule next to it, which is usually a separate, narrower set of hours — and which one applies to you depends entirely on what kind of campground you're standing in.

National parks, private chains like KOA, and BLM dispersed sites don't just have different quiet hours. They operate under different authorities, different enforcement levels, and in one case, almost no rules at all. Here's what's actually written down, what gets enforced in practice, and why a growing number of campers are sidestepping the whole question by not bringing a generator at all.

## Quiet Hours and Generator Hours Are Two Different Rules

This is the part most campers miss. "Quiet hours" cover all noise — voices, music, slamming doors. "Generator hours" are a separate, usually stricter, rule that applies specifically to engine-powered generators, and they're often shorter than the general quiet window.

A campground might have quiet hours from 10 PM to 6 AM but only allow generator use during two or three specific blocks during the day — meaning you could be in full compliance with quiet hours and still be in violation for running a generator during the wrong hour of the afternoon.

## National and State Parks: The Strictest, Most Specific Tier

National Park Service campgrounds tend to post the most detailed rules, and they vary park to park rather than following one nationwide standard.

At Yosemite and Sequoia & Kings Canyon, quiet hours run 10 PM to 6 AM, with generator use restricted to specific windows on top of that — commonly something like 8–11 AM and 5–8 PM, with some loops banning generators entirely regardless of time. Mount Rainier runs quiet hours from 9 PM to 7 AM with three separate generator windows (roughly 8–10 AM, noon–2 PM, and 5–7 PM), and bans generators outright in certain campground loops. Yellowstone is stricter still, with an 8 PM to 8 AM no-generator, no-loud-noise window.

The pattern across most national parks: a handful of two-hour generator windows during the day, a long overnight ban, and an increasing number of generator-free loops or sites reserved specifically for tent campers who don't want engine noise next door. National forests (a different federal agency, U.S. Forest Service) follow the same general shape but set their own hours forest by forest — there's no single rule that covers all of them.

## KOA and Private Campgrounds: It Depends on Who Owns That Location

KOA is a franchise, not a single operator — each of its 500+ locations is independently owned, and generator policy varies more here than anywhere else on this list. The quiet-hours range is fairly consistent (commonly 10 PM to 7 or 8 AM), but generator policy at the individual campground can be anything from "fine, just keep it under a noise limit" to "not allowed under any circumstances."

A meaningful number of full-hookup KOAs ban generators outright — not because of noise specifically, but because every site already has electric service, so there's no legitimate reason to run one. Others allow brief use outside quiet hours (one location's posted rule: up to 45 minutes, for things like topping off batteries). Where generators are allowed, a 60 dB noise limit gets cited often enough that it's become something close to an industry default — but it's set by each individual campground, not a KOA-wide corporate policy, so don't assume it applies until you check that specific location's rules.

## BLM Dispersed Camping: Minimal Rules, Maximum Etiquette

This is the loosest tier by a wide margin. The Bureau of Land Management oversees roughly 245 million acres, much of it open to dispersed camping — camping outside any developed, staffed campground. Because there's no campground host and often no posted signage at all, there are typically no established quiet hours and no generator-specific time windows.

The only real rule is the standard nuisance prohibition against "unreasonable noise" — broad, subjective, and rarely enforced unless someone files a complaint. The exception is BLM Special Recreation Management Areas near popular destinations, which sometimes carry additional restrictions closer to what you'd see at a developed campground. Outside of those, generator etiquette on BLM land runs almost entirely on social pressure and Leave No Trace norms rather than written rules — which is exactly why a loud generator at 6 AM in a dispersed camping area tends to generate dirty looks rather than a citation, but generates them all the same.

## The 60 Decibel Limit Everyone Cites — and Why It's Slipperier Than It Sounds

If you've researched this at all, you've run into the number 60 dB. It shows up constantly as "the" campground generator limit, attributed loosely to the National Park Service, to KOA, or just to campground rules in general.

The number itself is roughly accurate as a common ceiling — but the measurement distance behind it is inconsistent across sources and campgrounds. Some posted rules specify 50 feet, others reference 20 feet, and generator manufacturers typically publish their own ratings at 23 feet (7 meters), the standard testing distance for the industry. Sound drops off significantly with distance, so a generator that's "compliant" at 50 feet from the source could be well over the limit at 20 feet — meaning the same machine might pass at one campground and fail at the one next door, depending entirely on which distance gets enforced (if anyone's actually measuring at all, which in practice, most campground hosts aren't doing with a decibel meter).

For context on the scale itself: a quiet library runs around 30 dB, normal conversation around 60 dB, and the decibel scale is logarithmic — a 10 dB increase reads as roughly double the perceived loudness, not 10 units more. That's why the difference between a 55 dB inverter generator and a 75 dB conventional one isn't a 20-point gap on paper, it's the difference between barely noticeable and genuinely disruptive.

## Generator Noise vs. Solar/Battery Noise: The Actual Numbers

| Power source | Typical noise level | Real-world comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Conventional (open-frame) generator | 70–90 dB | Loud vacuum cleaner, busy street |
| Quiet inverter generator (light load) | 50–60 dB | Normal conversation |
| Quiet inverter generator (full load) | 55–65 dB | Conversation to busy street |
| Portable power station / solar generator | 20–50 dB | Quiet room to soft conversation |

The gap matters less in marketing copy and more at 6 AM when you're trying not to be the reason your neighbor's trip got worse. A battery-based power station has no combustion engine — there's nothing producing noise unless a cooling fan kicks on under heavy load, which on most units stays well under any posted limit. It's also the only option on this list that can't fail a decibel test, because there's effectively nothing to measure.

## Why Solar Is Winning Campground Culture

This isn't just a noise preference playing out — there's real regulatory and structural pressure pushing campers away from gas generators specifically.

California's Air Resources Board has been phasing in stricter emissions standards for small off-road engines (the category gas generators fall into) since 2024, with new portable generators required to meet zero-emission standards for sale starting in 2028. That's a meaningful signal for an industry that sells nationally — manufacturers don't build different generators for different states if they can help it.

On the ground, the shift shows up in three ways: more full-hookup campgrounds banning generators outright since shore power makes them redundant, more national park loops designated generator-free, and camping etiquette content across the board defaulting to "bring a solar setup" as the noise-compliant answer rather than "bring a quiet generator." Add the practical case — no fuel to haul, no carbon monoxide risk, nothing that can fail a decibel check because it doesn't combust anything — and the rules themselves are doing a lot of the persuading for you.

None of this means a generator is the wrong call for every situation — high, sustained power draw still favors fuel in some setups. But for the noise question specifically, the trend in what campgrounds allow and what campers are choosing both point the same direction.
